## 1. Our world as a MAPF instance (the facts both ideas have to fit)

### 1.1 Graph, clock, footprint

- **Graph.** A pointy-top hex lattice, pitch = `nominal_speed_mps · dt_s` = 120 m, so one lateral hop is exactly one step ([hexgrid.py](../freespace_sim/planner/hexgrid.py)). The density worlds are 60 × 30 km → **~144,300 cells per flight level**, and they fly **one** cruise level (100 m); the `_3lvl` variants add 80/95/110 m with ±7 m tubes, and a level change is a multi-step edge (`rung_steps ≥ 2`).
- **Clock.** `dt_s = 4`. A 2 h planner envelope is 1,800 steps, but the realised traffic is shorter: departures span **593–597 steps** and the unimpeded makespan (first departure → last nominal landing) is **741–744 steps** on both density worlds *[this session]*.
- **Footprint in time (the "multiple cells at once").** A corridor box is the swept hex cell padded by `time_buffer_s` on both sides, so a visit at step *v* claims the cell for rows **v−2 … v+1** — four periods (`derive_cell_window` → `(-2, 1)` on `main`). Two flights may share a cell only if their visits are **≥ 4 steps (16 s) apart**. In MAPF vocabulary that is a **k-robust vertex conflict with k = 3**, not a large-agent (multi-vertex) conflict: the plan's lemma that a hop box lies inside the union of its two cells' hexes means conflicting lattice hops always share an endpoint cell, so *same-cell-within-the-window* is the exact conflict predicate for lattice-aligned corridors. (The asymmetric "3-period" filing on `victor-qin/issue-84-labels-memory` drops this to k = 2 / 12 s; it is not on `main`.)
- **Footprint in space.** Takeoff and landing reserve a hover cylinder for `hover_time_s + climb` = 46.7 s ≈ **12 steps**; on `main` that cylinder claims **~9 cap-1 cells** (`endpoint_claim_cells` reach = 189 m; the #101 fix on `issue-101-endpoint-dwell` takes it to 129 m / ~4.2 cells). This is the dominant conflict type in practice: loading A\*'s own accepted schedule into colgen's rows, 63% of over-cap rows were dwell×transit and 36% dwell×dwell, transit×transit 1.2% *[memory, #101]*.
- **Terminals.** 182 / 476 hubs, **40 pads** each (a counting constraint on concurrent dwells, `TerminalCapacity`), radius 180 m, **15 exit lanes** on the boundary ring, and the whole column is a **permanent wall** for foreign traffic (`terminal_airspace_always_active`). A takeoff edge is "ground → lane cell at `s + takeoff_steps + lane_steps`" — a multi-step edge into a choice of 15 cells.
- **Costs.** One currency, per second: ground delay 1, lateral air 3, air hold 3, climb 4 ([config.py](../freespace_sim/config.py)). A\*'s edge types are exactly these four (ground wait reserves nothing; hover reserves the cell).

Three things matter here. First, the per-arrival joint problem is *small* at FAA density — the new flight plus two others typically, ≤ 3 for 86.5% of arrivals, never more than 11 — and **moderate at future density**: a median of 9, a p90 of 19, a maximum of 36. The FAA case is well inside the range where even *optimal* k-robust CBS is sub-second (§3.1); the future case is past the optimal-CBS cliff and squarely in PBS/LNS-repair territory (tens of A\* calls per arrival), which is why the scaling section treats the two worlds differently. The growth is sub-linear up to FAA (×2.5 in the mean for ×4.8 in concurrency — the conflicts are radial: endpoint dwells and lane cells near the flight's own hubs) and then roughly proportional (×5.1 for ×5.3 from FAA to future), i.e. above FAA density the hub funnels start interacting. Second, the partners are **strategically re-plannable** at every density — committed intents whose aircraft have not left the pad (98.9–99.5% of them), so a re-file is an ordinary strategic-phase update, not a tactical reroute. Third, the FCFS premium over unimpeded plans grows steeply with density — **11% → 19% → 68% of total cost** — and at future density FCFS is holding 95% of flights for a mean of six minutes: that is the dense-choke-point regime in which the online-MAPF literature measures 10–22% gains for joint replanning, not the ~1% of sparse maps. The colgen bound (§1.3) says only a part of the FAA premium is removable, since unimpeded plans conflict with each other; no comparable bound exists yet at future density.

### 1.5 Integration seams that already exist

- `sim.run` has exactly two execution shapes: the per-flight FCFS loop and the whole-schedule batch (`plans_whole_schedule`, used by colgen) — idea 1 would enter through the second, idea 2 through the first.
- The **Track A parallel runner** ([parallel.py](../freespace_sim/parallel.py)): speculative planning against a ledger snapshot with a recorded *read envelope* and ordered commit; exact mode is byte-identical to sequential. It *loses* today for plain A\* because the per-flight plan is ~100 ms and the serial commit floor dominates *[memory: parallel-loses-on-density-scenarios]* — the ratio flips once a per-arrival solve costs seconds, which is idea 2's regime.
- The compiled A\* kernel already takes a per-plan overlay (`ov_own_gen`, the own-column mark) and an `own` exclusion set; a per-agent **constraint overlay** of `(q, r, L, step-range)` cells — what a CBS/PBS low level needs — is the same mechanism with the opposite sign.
- Colgen's row model (`column_claims`, `RestrictedMaster.is_claim_feasible`, `complete_selection` = pin-and-refill) is a ready-made *discrete* conflict detector in cell-row terms, and `intent_to_column` maps A\* intents into it (with the known caveat that an A\* air hold — a repeated cell — is not representable as a column).
- `ReservationLedger.release(fid)` exists but rebuilds the whole index; `HexOccupancyService`/`CompiledHexOccupancy` are add-only (a release triggers a from-scratch rebuild with a warning). Incremental un-commit is the one piece of plumbing both ideas need that does not exist.

## 2. Idea 1 — LaCAM / LaCAM\* as the joint solver

### 2.1 What the literature actually shows *[lit]*

(Full survey with quotes in the appendix; the load-bearing facts only.)

- **LaCAM** (Okumura, AAAI 2023) is a DFS over *configurations* (the tuple of every agent's vertex) with lazy successor generation: each search node carries a small constraint tree ("agent *i* goes to *v* next"), and **PIBT** — one-timestep prioritized planning with priority inheritance and backtracking — generates one successor configuration per expansion. Complete for one-shot MAPF by exhaustion. Demonstrated: 400 agents on a 32×32 map in a median of 1 s; **10,000 agents on a 38k-vertex warehouse in ≤ 30 s**. The paper itself says it "compromises the quality compared to PP, EECBS, and LNS2".
- **LaCAM\*** (IJCAI 2023) keeps the goal node, stores `g`-values and neighbour sets, and runs a local Dijkstra rewrite whenever a known configuration is regenerated; proven complete and **eventually optimal** for any objective of the form `cost(Π) = Σ_t cost_e(Π[t], Π[t+1])` with `cost_e : V^|A| × V^|A| → R≥0`. Makespan, sum-of-fuels and sum-of-loss are instances; **flowtime (sum-of-costs) is not** ("history-dependent"), except via virtual goals. Solved 99% of 13,900 benchmark instances (≤ 1,000 agents) within 10 s; "the convergence speed was slow in large instances… finding new connections between known configurations becoming rare".
- **Engineering LaCAM\* / lacam3** (AAMAS 2024) adds random restarts, scattered-path guidance (SUO), Monte-Carlo PIBT (parallel threads), **dynamic incorporation of externally refined solutions** (MAPF-LNS-style: replan 1–30 random agents with PP + SIPP, others frozen; 4 threads) and recursion. ~30% cost reduction on a 409-agent dense map in 10 s; on 800 max-agent benchmark instances 799/800 solved vs LNS2's 510/800. At **10,000 agents** (5 min) only the scattered-path trick helped; "it remains challenging to refine solution quality effectively". The cost/LB ratio stays 1.5–1.7 there, ≈2–3 on dense random maps, ≈1.0–1.15 on sparse city maps.
- **PIBT** (IJCAI 2019 / AIJ 2022): ~10 ms per timestep at 10,000 agents; *reachability* (every agent eventually reaches its goal) is proved when every edge lies on a simple cycle — **true on an obstacle-free hex lattice**, which is a genuine point in PIBT's favour over 4-grids; incomplete for one-shot MAPF (livelock).
- **Extensions that matter to us, and their ceilings:** multi-cell / large agents — MD-PIBT (4×4/5×5 agents among 1×1, hundreds of agents, no LaCAM layer), HetPIBT (sizes + speeds, hundreds), db-LaCAM (continuous footprints, ≤ 50 robots); follower/rear-end conflicts (the k-robust analogue) — MAWPF (2026) needs a *multi-step* PIBT inside LaCAM and "consistently solved instances with several hundred agents"; vanilla PIBT "can hardly solve" it. Lifelong/online — RT-LaCAM (re-roots one DFS as agents move; needs bidirectional graphs), LLLG (windowed LaCAM + local guidance, 10k agents < 1 s/step), and the League of Robot Runners 2023 winner, which is **PIBT + parallel windowed MAPF-LNS + guidance graphs, not LaCAM**. **No release-time / earliest-start modelling exists in any one-shot LaCAM**; no GPU implementation of LaCAM or PIBT exists; all parallelism is CPU threads in the PIBT Monte-Carlo and the refiners.
- **Failure modes the papers own up to:** quality (the MAPF Tracker study finds vanilla LaCAM's solution quality "dominated by LNS2 on every domain"); corridor livelock; heavy dependence on the PIBT heuristic (a 7%-noisy heuristic made LaCAM 100× worse); slow LaCAM\* convergence and search-stuck after the first solution; bounded-suboptimal and better-heuristic variants "remain an open question"; windowed LaCAM\* refinement *reduced* lifelong throughput in LLLG.
- **Code:** `Kei18/lacam`, `lacam2`, `lacam3`, `lacam0`, `pibt2` — C++17, MIT; `lacam3` has a pybind branch; the graph loader is hard-wired 4-connected, the algorithms are graph-generic.

### 2.2 Mapping our world onto LaCAM\* — what each piece would take

1. **Objective.** Define `cost_e(X, Y) = Σ_i w_i(X[i], Y[i])` with `w = 1·dt` for a ground-wait transition before departure, `3·dt` for every airborne transition (move or hover), `4·Δz/climb_rate` for a rung, and **0 once the agent is parked at its goal and stays** (otherwise every parked flight accrues cost until the last flight lands and the objective becomes makespan-dependent). That is sum-of-loss with weights — inside the theorem. "Parked at goal" and "waiting on the pad before departure" are distinguishable because ground and air are different vertices (the ground state is its own vertex class), so no extra per-agent flag is needed. Admissible `h`: `3·dt·hex_distance(Q[i], g_i)` summed over airborne agents (+ the fixed climb terms) — exact for our unit-pitch lattice, so no per-goal Dijkstra is required. The PIBT preference order (`dist` to goal) is a monotone transform of the air cost, so PIBT needs no rescoring *for airborne moves*; what it cannot express is the ground-vs-air trade (next bullet).
2. **Release times and the pre-departure ground state.** One-shot LaCAM requires every agent to occupy a vertex at every step from t = 0. The clean encoding is the one already sketched in the chat: a private **ground chain** per flight (one vertex per step until `t_departure`, forward moves free, waiting charged 1·dt), feeding the hub's takeoff edge. Ground vertices never conflict with anything (they are not airspace), so they add no contention — but they add **~24,876 × ~170 chain vertices** to the graph in the future world and, more importantly, they make the solution **~744 configurations deep**. Published LaCAM instances resolve in a few hundred steps; depth by itself is not a cost problem (parked agents are free) but it is a *search* problem — every step of the DFS is a new configuration of N agents, and LaCAM\*'s refinement works by re-encountering configurations, which becomes rare as depth × N grows. The alternative is the lifelong framing (agents appear at their release time; windowed/receding-horizon LaCAM), which is what every published online use does — and which gives up the joint-over-the-whole-window optimisation that was the point.
3. **The k-robust footprint.** PIBT's one-step rule must refuse a cell that *any* agent occupied in the last 3 steps (a recently-vacated table) — that is sufficient for the symmetric window because moves are simultaneous. It is an easy change to the generator, but it is precisely the "follower conflict" that MAWPF (2026) reports vanilla PIBT cannot cope with without a 5–7 step lookahead inside LaCAM, at several hundred agents. MD-PIBT's diagnosis is structural: PIBT's backtracking handles a single dependency per agent; anything that couples several agents at once (a multi-cell dwell footprint, a cap-40 pad count, a lane cell blocked for four periods) defeats it.
4. **Endpoint dwell and pad capacity.** The 12-step, 9-cell landing/takeoff footprint is a multi-vertex occupation; pad capacity is a counting constraint over a *set* of agents. Neither is a vertex/swap conflict. They can be folded into the configuration-generator as extra feasibility checks (the generator may return ⊥ and let the constraint tree branch), which keeps LaCAM's completeness argument (the generator only needs to be able to enumerate all connected configurations) but removes the property that makes LaCAM fast — that PIBT almost always returns a good successor on the first try.
5. **Exit lanes and multi-step edges.** A takeoff is a ≥ 2-step edge into one of 15 lane cells; a rung is a ≥ 2-step edge. LaCAM assumes unit-duration transitions; the standard fix is dummy vertices on the edge (the graph grows, nothing breaks).
6. **Scale arithmetic** *[this session]*: one configuration is 19 KB (FAA) / 100 KB (future); a full-depth path 14 / 74 MB; 10k explored configurations 0.2 / 1.0 GB; a single PIBT pass over the horizon is ~24 M / ~130 M candidate evaluations (seconds in C++). Memory and a first feasible pass are not the problem at 5k, and probably not at 25k; the DFS revisit count and the footprint extension are the unknowns, and 25k exceeds every published LaCAM experiment.
7. **Parallelism on the supercomputer.** LaCAM's high-level search is a single-threaded DFS. The published parallelism is Monte-Carlo PIBT (k threads propose successors) and 1–4 refiner threads running MAPF-LNS on the incumbent. A cluster buys more refiners, which is — again — MAPF-LNS.

### 2.3 What LaCAM\* would likely deliver here

- **From scratch:** the initial solution comes from PIBT, which moves every agent toward its goal as early as possible. In our cost model that means *take off immediately and hover/detour in the air at 3×* rather than wait on the pad at 1×, and never hold a flight on the ground to let a crossing flight through — the opposite of what the 600-flight optimum does (57.8% held). The literature's cost/LB ratios (1.5–3 on congested maps, 1.0–1.15 on sparse ones) are for unit costs with a cost-aware generator; with a cost-blind generator and our asymmetry the initial gap would be larger. A\* FCFS sits 15.6% above the certified LB and most of that is unavoidable, so **a from-scratch LaCAM\* schedule would almost certainly be worse than A\* FCFS**, and LaCAM\*'s own refinement — slow at 1,000 agents, "challenging" at 10,000 — would not close it in any budget we would run.
- **Warm-started from A\* FCFS:** `lacam3`'s `IncorporateNewSolution` can splice an external schedule into the search graph as the incumbent (Dijkstra updates preserve the optimality structure). Then the configuration search contributes little — it needs to *re-encounter* configurations on the incumbent path to rewrite them, which at depth 744 × N = 4,636 essentially never happens — and all improvement comes from the refiner threads: replan a random 1–30 agents with PP + SIPP against the frozen rest, accept if cheaper. That is MAPF-LNS, whose repair operator in our world is the compiled A\* kernel, and whose destroy heuristics are well studied (RandomWalk-style neighbourhoods of 10–30 agents, PP repair beating PBS repair on time-efficiency by ~20× iterations/minute, delay reductions of 52–96% over the initial solution within 300 s at 350–900 agents; ADDRESS (AAAI-25) reports ≥ 50% cost improvement over MAPF-LNS at up to 1,000 agents; DROP-LNS parallelises destroy/repair on 8 threads up to 3,000 agents) *[lit]*. Those delay reductions are against *prioritized-planning* initial solutions on congested 2D maps, i.e. they measure exactly the FCFS-order inefficiency; in our sparse-mid-air / hub-bound world the analogous slice is the 6–15% above.
- **Where a configuration-space view would genuinely help:** the hub funnel — 15 lanes × 4-period cells × a 40-pad count × radial crossings — is the one place where "who goes first" is a joint decision and FCFS order is arbitrary. But that is a *local* joint problem (tens of flights in a hub-time window), and a local PBS/CBS/LNS solves it without a global configuration search. That is idea 2.

### 2.4 Verdict on idea 1, and the cheapest way to check it

Not recommended as a planner. If a number is wanted before closing #17: a **1–2 day spike** — export one 500–1,000-flight window of `density_faa` as a MAPF instance (ground chains for release times, W = 1, no dwell footprints, no pad cap; i.e. strictly easier than the real problem), run `lacam3` with a weighted sum-of-loss `cost_e` (a ~50-line edit of `get_edge_cost` + the PIBT history table), and compare the weighted cost of (a) LaCAM\*'s first solution, (b) LaCAM\* after 60 s, against A\* FCFS on the same window in the same currency. If (a) is more than ~10% worse than A\* on the *stripped* instance, the footprint and capacity extensions cannot save it and the issue can be closed with evidence.

## 3. Idea 2 — CBS-style local replanning when a flight arrives

### 3.1 What the literature shows *[lit]*

(Full citations in Appendix B; the load-bearing facts only.)

**CBS itself, and why its limits do not bite here.** CBS (Sharon et al., AAAI 2012 / AIJ 2015) is exponential in the number of *conflicts resolved in the constraint tree*, not in the number of agents in the world. With every accelerator since (cardinal conflicts, WDG heuristics, rectangle/corridor/target symmetry reasoning, disjoint splitting, mutex propagation) optimal CBS reaches 100% success at roughly 20–80 agents on congested 32×32 maps and 100–200 on large sparse maps in a minute; BCP (branch-and-cut-and-price) is in the same range. The bounded-suboptimal ECBS/EECBS buy 5–10× more agents (EECBS: provably ≤ 2% suboptimal at up to 1,000 agents on large maps in 1 min) — but EECBS still solved only ~49% of the 13,900-instance benchmark where LaCAM\* solved 99%. For idea 2 none of this matters directly: the tree contains the arrival plus its conflict set (§1.4), not the world. Two caveats do transfer: (a) **symmetry reasoning is unit-cost/4-connected specific** and would have to be re-derived for a weighted hex lattice (rectangle reasoning assumes Manhattan-optimal paths; MDD-width cardinality assumes level = timestep = cost); (b) **cheap ground holds create a departure-order symmetry** — the Flatland 2020 winners found CBS "exhaustively explores all possible departure sequences" when dozens of trains share a start cell, which is exactly our hub-fed arrivals. Neither breaks correctness (the proofs need only an optimal low level under constraints, node cost = sum of path costs, disjunctive branching — all fine with 1:3 costs, as CCBS/E-ICTS/CBS-NIC show); they break the accelerators.

**Multi-timestep and multi-cell conflicts cost an order of magnitude each — for *optimal* CBS.** MC-CBS for large agents (Li et al., AAAI 2019) solves 2–8 agents optimally (a plain CBS constraint moves a large agent one cell and it still overlaps; MC-CBS branches on mutually-disjunctive constraint *sets*); k-robust CBS (Atzmon et al., SoCS 2018 / JAIR 2020 — a k-delay conflict is π_i(t) = π_j(t+Δ), Δ ≤ k, resolved with range constraints ⟨agent, v, [t, t+k]⟩) is ~10× slower per unit of k on small grids (32×32, 30 agents: 42/50 solved at k = 0, 4/50 at k = 2); continuous-time CCBS (Andreychuk et al., IJCAI 2019 / AIJ 2022) handles ~10–25 agents; TR-CCBS (delay-robust continuous) collapses beyond 16–20 agents. Our footprint is the k = 3 range constraint plus a 12-step dwell footprint — natural in this vocabulary (the constraint is exactly the `(q, r, L, step-range)` overlay the kernel would take), but it argues for PBS/LNS-style repair rather than optimal CBS whenever the local set exceeds a handful.

**Online MAPF: how much does re-planning committed agents buy?** This is the literature that bounds idea 2, and it is sobering in sparse settings and encouraging at choke points:
- Švancara, Vlk, Stern, Atzmon, Barták (AAAI 2019): *Replan Single* (FCFS — the new agent routes around frozen others) vs *Replan All* (snapshot-optimal) vs *SubID* (re-route only a bounded set). Small dense intersections, 10–25 agents: RA beats RS by **10–22%** sum-of-costs; a larger sparse grid, 60–70 agents: **1–1.5%**. The strong result for us: **SubID re-routes ~5 agents where RA re-routes ~30 and gets "virtually the same" cost** — steep diminishing returns in the number of committed agents moved.
- Morag, Stern, Felner (SoCS 2021/2022): snapshot-optimal equals oracle-optimal (future arrivals known) in 82–98% of instances; Replan Single is within **0.01–2% of oracle-optimal** on standard maps (worst 3.3%, 12.5% in a maze); "solving sub-optimally while completely avoiding reroutes produced higher quality solutions under most reroute costs" once a re-route has a price.
- RHCR (Li et al., AAAI 2021): windowed replan-all gains **4–7% throughput** over replan-new-only methods at 60–140 agents in a dense warehouse — at 30–200× the runtime per call; bounded horizons (w = 5 vs ∞) change throughput < 3% while scaling from 700 to 1,000+ agents.
- Flatland 2020 (Li et al., ICAPS 2021), trains arriving over time: partial replanning of only the trains that share future intersections cut flowtime **19.9%** on 261/400 instances; LNS over PP another 12.4%; 4-thread parallel LNS 11.1% more. PUSH (2026) replans ~20% of 10,000 agents per step within 1 s.
- **UTM, the closest setting (Ho, Geraldes, Gonçalves, Cavazza, Prendinger et al., AAMAS 2019 / IEEE Access 2019 / T-ITS 2020)**: 30 m voxels, 478×570×2, ~200 UAVs airborne, 0.3% occupancy, Sendai 2030 demand (13.9–21.2k ops/day); conflicts are continuous-time capsule overlaps; **accepted operations are never modified**. Batched ECBS(1.5) vs CA\*-FCFS at (700 accepted, 500 new): 104 s vs 131 s, mean deviation **42 m vs 51 m** (a few seconds of flight), rejections **0.5% vs 2%**; CBS times out from 400 accepted. Their own conclusion: batching helps when requests bunch; "if requests drop in with low frequency, CA\*-based is clearly more suitable". Conflicts are mostly intra-hub/intra-USSP — the same radial structure as ours. The decentralised follow-up (4 USSPs, 1,000 ops/h) resolves cross-operator conflicts by cost-based prioritisation or pairwise negotiation in < 5 min.
- The only clean **FCFS vs global-optimum delay number for drones** is Tang, Xu, Inalhan (SESAR 2021): 200 flights / 24 h, 15-min separations, ground delay only — FCFS 1,215 min vs full MILP 735 min (**−39.5%**) at 1,320 s vs 5 s; batches of ~40 flights −9%. Coarse and tiny, but the direction and the batch-size curve are the ones to expect.
- **Cost convention**: the ATFM lineage (Bertsimas–Stock Patterson 1998; Bertsimas–Lulli–Odoni 2011) prices airborne delay above ground delay, and the UTM adaptation by Chin, Gopalakrishnan, Balakrishnan, Egorov, Evans (ICRAT 2020 / T-ITS 2021) uses **α = 3** with a battery cap on airborne holds — our 1:3 is the accepted convention, not an oddity. MAPF papers, by contrast, charge a "garage" wait like any other wait; the asymmetry is bolted on, never native.
- **Hub saturation under ground-delay-only deconfliction** (Evans, Egorov, Munn, AIAA 2020): one operator at 250 req/h/hub already sees ~6 min mean ground delay; with a 30-min file-ahead asymmetry the late filer waits > 1 h 45 min. That is the regime our lead arms probe, and it says the contestable delay lives at the hubs.

**The repair operator everyone converges on.** MAPF-LNS (Li et al., IJCAI 2021): destroy 4–16 agents (agent-based random walk from the most-delayed agent, map-based intersections, random), repair with **prioritized planning** — PP beats CBS/EECBS as the repair operator on 74% of instances because it is 10–100× faster per iteration; cuts a PP start's sum of delays by up to 36× on congested maps, lands within 1.35% of optimal where optimal is known, and the initial solution barely matters. The SoCS 2024 re-evaluation confirms PP over PBS on time-efficiency (8.98k vs 0.44k iterations per minute), RandomWalk-style destroy as the best heuristic, and **neighbourhood size as the under-explored knob worth ~50%**. MAPF-LNS2 (AAAI 2022) repairs *infeasible* plans (3,000 agents in 1 min, 8,000 in 1 h); ADDRESS (AAAI 2025) improves MAPF-LNS by ≥ 50% at up to 1,000 agents with an adaptive delay-based destroy; DROP-LNS parallelises destroy/repair (best at 8 threads, up to 3,000 agents); CPLNS reports a median 21.95× speed-up on 32 cores at up to 10,000 agents. The UTM instance of the same recipe is Sow et al. (Linköping/UNICAMP, AAMAS 2026, code `amathsow/4DPlanning`): prioritised planning by departure urgency + soft-conflict SIPP + an LNS repair loop over the geometric collision graph (neighbourhood ≤ 10): 100% success at 1,000 UAVs on a 100×100×10 voxel world; city-scale 400×400×25: 100% to 200 agents at 401 s, 30% at 500 with four NFZs under a 1,200 s cap; batch CBS "fails to scale beyond small instances", plain PP "fails beyond 250 agents" *as a feasibility method*.

Read together: the literature's answer to "does re-planning committed flights when a new one arrives beat FCFS?" is **~1% in sparse traffic, 10–22% at dense choke points, and most of it is recoverable by moving a handful of agents, with PP/LNS-style repair rather than an optimal tree**. Our world is sparse mid-air and dense at the hubs, so the gain should concentrate exactly where the lead arms say the delay is.

### 3.2 The local instance, concretely

When flight *f* files:

1. Plan *f* against the committed ledger exactly as today → the FCFS plan with cost `c_f^FCFS` (this is the fallback and the acceptance baseline).
2. Plan *f* **unimpeded** (static walls only) → `c_f^0`. If `c_f^FCFS − c_f^0` is below a threshold (36% of arrivals have no conflicting partner at all; 42% of flights take zero ground delay even in the 600-flight optimum), commit the FCFS plan and stop.
3. Collect the conflict set **C** = earlier-committed flights whose reservations the unimpeded plan hits (`ledger.conflicts`, filtered to flights that have **not departed** — measured 99.5% of partners at 21% density). Expand at most one hop in the conflict graph if a partner's alternatives are themselves blocked.
4. Solve the local joint problem over `{f} ∪ C` with everyone else frozen, objective `Σ cost` in the run's currency (each flight's cost measured against its own `t_departure`), and **accept only if strictly cheaper than `c_f^FCFS + Σ_C c_g^current`**. Two acceptance policies are worth carrying as arms from day one: *utilitarian* (any net saving) and *Pareto* (no committed flight's cost may rise — i.e. only free lateral swaps and free re-timings of others are allowed). The Pareto arm is the one that stays closest to FCFS's promise and is the one #105's data says captures most of the value (84.4% of the alternatives are delay-identical swaps).
5. Re-file: release the partners' old reservations, commit the new set (through `FCFSMechanism.commit` so the re-check stays the single authority), and record the event (which partners moved, by how much) for the fairness readout.

The search in step 4 does not need full CBS. Two cheaper shapes cover it: **PBS over the local set** (branch on priority order of `{f} ∪ C`, low level = our A\* with the others as obstacles — PBS is near-optimal and ~20× cheaper per iteration than CBS in the LNS re-evaluation), or the **min-conflict escalation** of #105 (each partner tries a zero-cost geodesic swap that clears *f*; then *f* tries again; then pay: ground delay, then +1 hop). Full CBS with k-robust constraints is the exact fallback if a cardinal conflict cannot be resolved by swaps; with |C| ≤ 3 its tree is trivially small.

### 3.3 Why this matches the measured structure

- The local problem is tiny (§1.4) and the partners are on the ground (§1.4), so there is no tactical/strategic boundary problem.
- The lattice gives each flight an enormous tie class of equal-cost routes (97.8% of flights have many geodesics, median 10^19; the geodesic DAG's widest layer is median 20 cells) *[memory, #105]*, so "move someone sideways for free" almost always exists.
- A\* already takes the free swap for *itself* when one exists (61.6% of the time it then departs on time); what it cannot do is move a *committed* flight sideways — and when no swap is available to the newcomer it holds on the ground 89.7% of the time *[memory, #105]*. The whole mechanism of idea 2 is to give the newcomer that one missing move.
- The 600-flight optimum shows what the end state looks like: 62% of flights on a non-A\* geodesic, 58% held, only 18% unchanged from A\*'s filing — a pattern local replanning can approach one arrival at a time, without the LP/IP machinery.

### 3.4 Scaling to 5k and 25k, with a supercomputer

- **Per-arrival cost.** Each low-level call is one A\* plan (55–136 ms compiled; more for the high-delay outbound tail, which is exactly the tail that enters step 4). The CBS survey's order-of-magnitude budget by local-set size, with our kernel as the low level: ≤ 5 interacting flights — optimal CBS-family 1–100 ms-equivalent node counts (i.e. a few to a few dozen A\* calls), PP/PBS/LNS repair a handful of calls; ~10 — optimal k-robust/MC-style CBS 0.1–10 s with success 0.5–0.95 in a minute, bounded-suboptimal 10 ms–1 s, LNS repair ~10–20 calls; ~20 — optimal frequently fails, ECBS 0.1–10 s, LNS 10–50 calls; 50–200 — only PBS/LNS. So: a local PBS/CBS over |C|+1 ≤ 4 agents is ~1 s of kernel time; at future density (measured |C| mean 9.9, p90 19, max 36) the tree must be bounded — depth-limited PBS, or the #105 escalation with a node budget — to stay at ~5–20 s, and optimal CBS is off the table for the p90 tail. Arithmetic (measured: 86.4% of FAA arrivals have ≥ 1 conflicting partner, but only the 75.7% that FCFS actually delays need a solve — the rest already fly unimpeded; at future 97.8% have a partner and 94.9% are delayed): FAA, ~65–75% of arrivals needing a solve at 1–5 s → **0.8–4.5 h sequential**; future, ~95% at 5–20 s → **33–130 h sequential** on top of the 1.5 h the plain FCFS A\* run itself took today. Ideal 64-way parallelism takes those to 1–4 min and 0.5–2 h; the published parallel-LNS numbers (DROP-LNS saturating at 8 threads per instance, CPLNS median 21.95× on 32 cores as a portfolio) say the cores should go to *independent neighbourhoods*, not to one tree.
- **How to parallelise.** The Track A speculative runner already does the right thing — plan arrivals concurrently against a snapshot, commit in FCFS order, re-plan only those whose *read envelope* an interleaved commit invalidated — and it lost on plain A\* only because the plan was cheaper than the commit. With second-scale plans it wins; what changes is that the envelope must now also cover the *released* reservations of the partners (an un-commit is a write other speculations may have read), and exact mode becomes "byte-identical to the sequential version of this mechanism", not to FCFS. The alternative for 25k is hub-sharding: conflict sets are radial, so arrivals at hubs whose 16 km service disks are time-disjoint are independent, and a cluster job can run hub groups in parallel with a cross-shard reconciliation at the end (the same ordered-commit re-check). Both are engineering, not research.
- **Plumbing that does not exist:** incremental `release` in the ledger (today an O(N) rebuild), an "un-rasterise" path in `HexOccupancyService`/`CompiledHexOccupancy` (today add-only; a release forces a full rebuild), and `TerminalCapacity` interval removal. All three are the inverse of code that exists; the compiled occupancy pools are interval lists per cell, so removal is a splice.

### 3.5 Expected gain and how to read it

Bounded above by the joint-optimum headroom (§1.3): ~6–7% of weighted cost at 600–1,500 flights, presumably more at future density where FCFS order bites harder. A greedy one-arrival-at-a-time local optimiser will not reach the optimum. The online-MAPF literature (§3.1) brackets what it *does* reach: replan-all over FCFS is ~1% on sparse maps and 10–22% at dense choke points, Replan Single sits within 0.01–3.3% of the oracle on standard maps, and moving ~5 agents recovers what moving ~30 would. Our own colgen data adds that the value is in *combinations* (pool composition, not count). So the honest prior is **low single-digit % of weighted cost at FAA**, concentrated at the hubs — with the caveat that our contestable slice there is itself only 6–15% of the total because three-quarters of the cost is lattice tax — and **materially more at future density**, where FCFS holds 95% of flights for a mean of six minutes and its premium over unimpeded plans is 68% of cost: that is the dense-choke-point regime (Švancara's 10–22%, RHCR's 4–7% throughput, Flatland's 19.9%), and the lattice tax is no longer the dominant term. The measurement that decides it is cheap: run the Pareto arm on the 974-leg cut and on full FAA and difference per flight against the A\* run (same flight set, same currency), and report it next to the colgen IP number at 600 flights so the three sit on one ruler.

### 3.6 Risks and open design decisions

- **Mechanism, not planner.** Re-filing another flight's accepted intent is no longer FCFS. Within one USS it is the operator re-optimising its own fleet (clearly allowed); across USSs it is negotiation, which F3548 leaves to the operators. The run must record the arm (`fcfs` / `fcfs+swap-pareto` / `fcfs+swap-utilitarian`) in `experiment.json`/`index.parquet` exactly as `warm_start_planner` is recorded, or archived comparisons become unreadable — the same reporting trap #105 flags.
- **Termination / oscillation.** A partner moved for flight *f* can later be moved back for flight *h*. Needs a strictly decreasing potential (total cost, or per-flight move budget/tabu); the Pareto arm is monotone by construction, the utilitarian arm is not.
- **Exactness contract of the parallel runner** changes as described above; the sequential version of the mechanism must be the oracle.
- **Determinism.** Tie-breaking among equal-cost swaps must be seeded and recorded so that runs are reproducible across machines (colgen's pricing already is not bit-identical across machines because of wall-clock cutoffs — avoid wall-clock budgets inside the local search).
- **A\* air holds** are not representable as colgen columns; if the trace is to feed colgen (per #105), holds must either be kept out of the replanned partners or the column model extended.

### 3.7 Verdict on idea 2

Spike it, in this order: (1) the incremental release plumbing (needed by anything that ever moves a committed flight, LNS included); (2) the Pareto local-swap arm with depth-1 push, measured on the 974-leg cut against A\*; (3) the utilitarian arm; (4) the Track A runner on top. The same operator run *offline* over the finished A\* schedule — pick a hub-time neighbourhood, release its flights, replan them in a new order, accept if cheaper — is MAPF-LNS, and it is the batch counterpart of the same spike with the same plumbing; the literature says it is what LaCAM3, the Robot-Runners winner and the Linköping UTM planner all converge on.

## 4. Idea 3 — MAPF-LNS2 seeded by the A\* schedule: offline refinement, and an online repair that runs as each flight arrives

> Added 2026-08-22 (second pass) at Victor's request: "add LNS2 as a solution, using the A\* search as the initial solution; ideally an online LNS2 that repairs as new flights are added." This section is grounded in a full read of the LNS2 paper (Li, Chen, Harabor, Stuckey, Koenig, AAAI 2022) and its quality sibling MAPF-LNS (IJCAI 2021), plus the adjacent online/parallel LNS literature (Appendix B).

### 4.1 What MAPF-LNS2 actually is *[lit — read in full]*

- **Problem framing.** LNS2 does not start from a feasible plan. It takes whatever a MAPF algorithm produced — typically prioritized planning (PP) that *failed* for some agents — plans the missing agents so as to *minimise collisions* with the existing paths, and then repairs: repeatedly select a subset `A_s` of agents (the neighbourhood, **N = 8** in the paper; 4/8/16/32 behave similarly with no global winner), delete their paths, re-plan them **one at a time in a random priority order** with **SIPPS**, where higher-priority neighbourhood agents' new paths and all non-neighbourhood paths are *soft obstacles* (collide if you must, but minimise collisions), and **accept the new plan iff the number of colliding pairs (CP) does not increase**. Repeat until CP = 0 (feasible) or the time limit. The repair engine is PP because they tried Greedy CBS and PBS as the neighbourhood re-planner and "both perform worse than PP empirically" (footnote 1).
- **Neighbourhood selection (the part that matters most).** Three generators mixed by ALNS (weights updated with reaction factor γ = 0.1):
  - *collision-based*: pick a random vertex of the **collision graph** (agents = vertices, edges = colliding pairs) with degree > 0, take its largest connected component; if it has ≤ N agents, take them all and grow the set by letting a random member random-walk along its path until it collides with someone, who is added; otherwise random-walk on the collision graph itself to pick N;
  - *failure-based*: pick an agent `a_i` with probability ∝ its collision degree, then collect `A^s` (agents whose paths visit `a_i`'s start) and `A^g` (agents whose targets lie on `a_i`'s shortest path) — the two reasons PP fails ("boxed in at the start" and "run over at the goal") — filling to N with a preference for `A^g`;
  - *random*: N agents with probability ∝ degree + 1.
- **SIPPS (the engine).** Safe Interval Path Planning with Soft constraints: safe intervals are built from the *hard* obstacles only and **split further by soft obstacles**; a node is (vertex, interval, is_goal) with keys **c first, then f** — `c` counts soft collisions on the partial path (+1 if the node's interval contains a soft vertex/target obstacle, +1 per soft edge), `f = g + h` is travel time. Goal handling: a node at the target with `low ≥ T` (T = one past the last hard obstacle at the goal) is a goal only if no soft obstacles visit the goal afterwards (`c_future = 0`); otherwise a goal node carrying `c + c_future` is generated. Dominance is weakened to (same vertex, same interval id, same goal flag, interval ⊇, `c` ≤). Guarantees: complete (Thm 1); **returns a shortest path with zero soft collisions whenever one exists** (Thm 2); when none exists it may return more than the minimum number of collisions (measured: 95% of runs minimum, 4% one above). Measured **~5× faster than space-time A\* with soft constraints** (51 ms vs 524 ms per call on random-32-32-20 at 300–400 agents), and A\* with soft constraints can loop on the goal-waiting issue unless the time axis is bounded.
- **Results.** On random-32-32-20, PP with random restarts (PP^R) has success 0.56/0.08/0/0 at 100/200/300/400 agents; MAPF-LNS2 1.00/1.00/1.00/0.88, needing 105/262/1,285 SIPPS runs vs PP^R's 47,114 at 200. On all 33 benchmark maps at the largest agent counts, **> 60% solved within 1 min and 80% within 5 min** (PPS/EECBS/EECBS\*/PP^R all lower); failures are on maze/room maps and end with 1–2 colliding pairs; memory stays flat. Warehouse-20-40-10-2-2: **3,000 agents in a minute, 5,000 in 5 min, 8,000 in an hour**. They deliberately did not seed with EECBS/PPS: "whenever PP finds solutions, it always finds them faster than EECBS\*/PPS, and whenever it fails to find them, MAPF-LNS2 repairs the plan rapidly".
- **Code facts that matter for us** (`Jiaoyang-Li/MAPF-LNS2`, C++14, Boost/Eigen, USC research licence): the collision-repair loop (`InitLNS`) runs **only if the initial solver fails** — a feasible start goes straight to the MAPF-LNS improvement loop, and `--maxIterations` **defaults to 0**, so the stock command only repairs to feasibility; acceptance in the repair loop is CP **≤** (ties accepted); the collision-based random walk gives up after 10 failed walks; ALNS uses γ = 0.1 in the paper but 0.05 in the code; the low level is a **4-connected unit-cost grid** (`g_val = timestep`, BFS heuristics) with a **random tie-break inside the heap comparator**; the reservation/constraint tables already accept **time-range soft intervals** (`insertSoftConstraint2SIT(loc, t_min, t_max)`, `RANGE` constraints), so a k-robust inflation of each occupancy to [t−k, t+k] is a table change. None of it ships weighted/asymmetric costs or footprints — the engine has to be ours.
- **MAPF-LNS (the quality sibling, IJCAI 2021).** Feasible start (PP/PPS/EECBS); destroy = agent-based random walk from a most-delayed agent collecting the agents whose paths it meets, map-based (all agents through an intersection vertex), random; ALNS; **repair = PP with random order, others hard**; accept iff sum-of-costs decreases. Cuts a PP start's sum of delays by up to 36× in 60 s on congested maps, lands within 1.35% of optimal where known, and the initial solution barely matters (random-32-32-20/100 agents: starts 299/468/2,224 → finals 138/141/136). PP beats CBS/EECBS as the repair operator on 74% of instances because it is 10–100× faster per iteration. The SoCS 2024 re-evaluation (RandomWalk destroy best; neighbourhood size is the under-explored knob worth ~50%; PP > PBS at 8.98k vs 0.44k iterations/min), ADDRESS (AAAI 2025: restricted Thompson sampling over the top-K most-delayed agents, ≥ 50% better than MAPF-LNS at up to 1,000 agents), DROP-LNS (asynchronous destroy/repair, best at 8 threads, 3,000 agents) and CPLNS (median 21.95× on 32 cores, 10,000 agents) are the current frontier; the League of Robot Runners 2023 winner and LaCAM3's refiners are the same operator wrapped differently.

### 4.2 Why it fits this world — better than either idea above

1. **Our initial solution is already feasible and strong.** A\* FCFS is a prioritized-planning schedule with CP = 0, within 15.6% of colgen's certified bound at 2,000 flights. So "LNS2 seeded by A\*" is, in steady state, **MAPF-LNS** (improvement) — and LNS2's soft-constraint machinery is kept for two jobs where it is irreplaceable: repairing a neighbourhood re-plan that PP cannot place under the ground-delay/expansion budgets (instead of reverting), and — the point of the online variant — **planning a newcomer against committed flights as soft obstacles to find the smallest set it must disturb.**
2. **The conflict structure is LNS2's native habitat.** LNS2's collision-based neighbourhood is a connected component of the collision graph; our measured components are tiny at FAA (a new flight's unimpeded plan hits a mean of 1.9 committed flights, ≤ 3 for 86.5%) and moderate at future (mean 9.9, p90 19, max 36) — i.e. N = 8 neighbourhoods cover FAA components whole and slice future components the way LNS2 is designed to. The partners are ~99% still on the ground, so re-filing them is an ordinary strategic update.
3. **The footprint is interval-shaped, which is SIPP's representation.** A committed visit at step *v* occupies its cell for rows v−2 … v+1; the endpoint dwell occupies ~9 cells for ~12 steps; the compiled occupancy already stores exactly this as **per-cell interval lists** (`_packed.P_LO/P_HI/P_NXT`). SIPPS's safe intervals are that structure read the other way round, and a soft obstacle is simply an interval tagged with its owner. A compiled **SIPP** kernel already exists on `claude/sipp-compiled` (cost-exact vs A\* at moderate density, multi-altitude, ~7× faster than A\* on short hub flights *[memory: compiled-sipp-scaling]*); SIPPS = that kernel + a soft-interval pool + (c, f) ordering.
4. **The cost model is not a problem — and the hard part is already written.** Textbook SIPP's dominance ("arriving earlier in a safe interval dominates") is exact on our *air* graph because hovering and moving both cost 3·dt, but not across the cheaper ground wait (1·dt), which makes the departure step a genuine decision. The `claude/sipp-compiled` kernel (`sipp_kernel.py`, 2026-07-09) already solves exactly this: it is a **cost-aware** SIPP with a multi-label Pareto frontier per (cell, interval) — a stored label `(t2, g2)` dominates `(t, g)` iff `t2 ≤ t` and `g2 + (t − t2)·c_hold ≤ g` — and it enumerates ground delay into the start labels from the host's dwell/pad feasibility mask, so every in-air wait is an air hover at `c_hold`; compiled == reference == A\* multi-altitude. SIPPS on top of it is: a second, owner-tagged interval pool for soft obstacles, the soft-collision count `c` as the *primary* key of the label (then weighted cost), the goal rule with `c_future`, and the heuristic rewritten in cost units (`h = max{3·dt·dist, 3·(T − t)}`). The independent derivation in the LNS2 survey lands on the same design: with cost `= 3·T_arrive − 2·d_depart` two partial paths to the same (vertex, interval) with earlier arrival but later departure are *incomparable*, so the fix is a Pareto set over (arrival, departure) per (vertex, interval, c) — which is precisely the multi-label frontier the branch kernel already keeps. One refinement to adopt from SIPPS-WC (AAMAS 2026): SIPPS undercounts collisions incurred by *waiting inside* a soft interval; a waiting-conflict counter in the label fixes it at ~2× per-call cost. The cheaper stop-gap is the paper's own alternative: **the compiled A\* kernel plus a soft pool and a lexicographic (c, f) heap key** — "space-time A\* with soft constraints", correct but ~5× slower than SIPPS and needing the bounded time axis the kernel already has (`max_step`). Either way it is a mode, not a new planner.
5. **CP accounting is exact and cheap here.** "Colliding pairs" = pairs of flights sharing a (cell, row) claim or an FCL conflict; the ledger's `conflicts` returns partner ids exactly, and colgen's row model (`column_claims` / `is_claim_feasible`) gives the discrete version. The paper approximates CP inside the search (tracking exact pair sets "substantially increases the search space"); we can do the same and take the exact count on the candidate.
6. **Objective-agnostic acceptance.** LNS accepts on any scalar; the weighted currency `1·ground + 3·air` drops in, and a *Pareto* variant (no committed flight's cost may rise) is a per-agent cost cap inside the repair.

### 4.3 Form A — offline: LNS2/LNS over the finished A\* schedule (the batch counterpart of idea 2)

- **Loop.** Start from the A\* FCFS schedule (CP = 0). Destroy with three generators under ALNS: (a) *premium-based* — pick a flight with probability ∝ its FCFS premium (cost − unimpeded cost; ADDRESS's top-K Thompson sampling is the refinement) and random-walk along its **unimpeded** path collecting the flights whose reservations block it — this is exactly the conflict set measured in §1.4, and it is LNS's agent-based neighbourhood transplanted to a schedule; (b) *hub-window* — every flight using a hub's lanes/column over a time window (the map-based neighbourhood; our congestion is radial so this is the natural "intersection"); (c) random. N = 8–16 (tune; the re-evaluation says it matters ~50%). Repair = PP in a random priority order with the compiled A\* kernel, the rest **hard** (MAPF-LNS); if a flight cannot be placed within budget, switch that neighbourhood to soft (LNS2) and keep repairing until CP = 0, else revert. Accept iff Σ weighted cost strictly decreases (utilitarian) or iff it decreases with no flight worse (Pareto). Anytime; every repair's alternative routes are canonical columns — the column pool #105 wants.
- **Cost.** One iteration ≈ N kernel calls ≈ 8 × 0.05–0.14 s ≈ 0.4–1.1 s at FAA; thousands of iterations per run → minutes to an hour sequential; future: tens of hours sequential → parallel by construction: neighbourhoods are hub-local, so DROP-LNS-style asynchronous destroy/repair over disjoint neighbourhoods is near-linear until hubs share airspace, and CPLNS-style portfolios (random orders in parallel, keep the best) add another axis. On the cluster: shard by hub groups with cross-shard flights hard, exchange periodically.
- **Expected gain.** Bounded by the colgen headroom at FAA (6–15% of weighted cost, most of the rest lattice tax) — so a few %; at future density the FCFS premium is 68% of cost and FCFS is holding 95% of flights for a mean of 6 min, the regime in which the LNS literature's delay reductions (52–96% over PP starts, up to 36×) were measured. No bound exists yet at future; LNS would *produce* one (an incumbent).
- **Plumbing.** Incremental ledger release + occupancy un-raster + `TerminalCapacity` removal (the same three pieces idea 2 needs); a soft-pool mode in the kernel for the LNS2 escape (optional for the pure-LNS offline form).
- **The anytime contract (why this answers "return feasible improvements over time").** Seeded by A\* FCFS, the incumbent is a complete, ledger-feasible schedule from second zero, and the acceptance rule (replace only when a repaired neighbourhood is conflict-free *and* cheaper) keeps it feasible and monotonically non-increasing in cost at every instant — stop whenever you like and file the incumbent; every accepted repair is an emission point. This is MAPF-LNS's defining property, and it is why the LNS2 *collision* phase never touches the incumbent here: a neighbourhood that goes soft mid-repair is only folded back once it is collision-free again, or reverted. Two caveats and one bonus: there is **no suboptimality bound** (LNS is unboundedly suboptimal in theory; in practice within 1.35% of optimal where optimal is known); the alternatives that do carry bounds are worse fits — anytime BCBS/EECBS "fail to improve the initial solution substantially" and run out of memory at scale (measured in the MAPF-LNS paper), cannot start from an external schedule, and BCP-LNS (exact anytime) tops out around 100–200 agents. The bonus is repo-specific: **pair the LNS incumbent with colgen's LP lower bound for a live gap certificate** — at 2,000 flights the certified bound 233,972 already exists, so any incumbent can be reported as "+X% above a proven bound" while it improves; at future density LNS would produce the first real incumbent and a colgen LP pass the first bound.

### 4.4 Form B — online: newcomer-centred LNS2 repair on each arrival (the one Victor asked for)

Per arrival *f*, in FCFS order:

```
P_fcfs ← A*(f | committed ledger HARD)                     # today's plan; the fallback; cost c_fcfs
if c_fcfs − c_unimpeded(f) ≤ τ: commit P_fcfs; return     # FAA: ~24% of arrivals; future: ~5%
P_soft ← A*-soft / SIPPS(f | static walls + pad capacity HARD, committed reservations SOFT)
                                                          # minimise # of colliding partners, then weighted cost
C ← committed flights colliding with P_soft               # FAA mean 1.9 (max 11); future mean 9.9 (max 36)
C_hard ← flights in C already departed (~1%) → re-plan P_soft with them hard; C ← C \ C_hard
LNS2 on the sub-instance {f} ∪ C, everything else HARD:
    repeat: A_s ← collision-based neighbourhood (component of f, N ≤ 8)   # at FAA: the whole set
            re-plan A_s in random priority order, kernel soft mode, others soft
            accept iff CP does not increase
    until CP = 0 or an ITERATION budget (never wall-clock)
if CP = 0 and accept(new, fallback):                      # utilitarian: Σcost strictly lower;
    release partners' old reservations, re-file new ones, commit f   #  Pareto: no partner's cost rises
else commit P_fcfs
```

- **Why "soft plan first" — and why it is LNS2's own entry point.** `P_soft` is the newcomer's best claim on the airspace; the set it collides with is the minimum-disturbance neighbourhood. This is not an invention: LNS2's own first step "for each agent that does not yet have a path, plans a path for it that minimises the number of collisions with the existing paths" and then builds the collision graph from those collisions (`InitLNS::getInitialSolution` in the code) — Form B is that entry point applied one agent at a time, with the committed world frozen outside the component. The same move recurs as Flatland's "partial replanning of the trains that visit the disturbed train's future intersections" (−19.9% flowtime, PP within 3 s), Flatland-3's LNS partial replanning (r = 20 rounds × p = 20 iterations, accept iff total arrival delay drops), Morag's Fail@LH(R) (replan only agents with a conflict within R = 3–5 steps; larger R *hurts*), and DTAPP-IICR's collision-graph neighbourhood (N = 10, rollback on failure — though DTAPP itself is batch: all missions are sorted by departure urgency and planned in one pass; no published system does this online). Because SIPPS returns a *zero-collision* shortest path whenever one exists (Thm 2), `P_soft` coincides with the unimpeded plan exactly when the newcomer is unimpeded, and degrades gracefully otherwise. Two literature cautions transfer directly: gate the repair ("Should I Replan?", 2026, found blind replanning helps in only 7% of runs and a trigger recovered 94.6% of the value — our gate is the premium threshold τ), and bound how far the disturbance may propagate (Fail@LH's small R; Flatland's cap of ~600 trains).
- **What the repair typically does.** With 97.8% of flights having many equal-cost geodesics, the first thing a partner's re-plan finds under (c, f) ordering is a *zero-collision alternative of equal cost* — a free lateral swap — whenever one exists; only when none exists does a partner or the newcomer pay (ground delay at 1×, then detour at 3×). The Pareto arm makes that explicit with a per-partner cost cap; the utilitarian arm lets a partner pay when the sum drops.
- **Guarantees.** The FCFS plan is always the fallback, so the mechanism is never worse than FCFS per arrival; CP = 0 and the ledger re-check (`FCFSMechanism.commit` stays the single authority) preserve the no-conflict invariant; `verify` remains the backstop. Oscillation across arrivals is bounded by the Pareto cap (a flight's cost never rises) or, in the utilitarian arm, by a per-flight re-file budget/tabu; a **freeze horizon** (RTTA-style, Evans–Egorov–Munn: ~250 s kept fairness ≥ 0.95) keeps flights about to depart hard.
- **Cost per arrival.** FAA: one soft plan + a handful of repair calls ≈ 5–20 kernel calls ≈ 0.3–2 s; future: |C| ≈ 10 → N = 8 neighbourhoods × 5–20 iterations ≈ 40–160 calls ≈ 4–16 s → 25k × 95% × ~8 s ≈ 50 h sequential, i.e. the parallel runner or hub-sharding is a requirement (§3.4), and within an arrival a portfolio of random priority orders is embarrassingly parallel.
- **What it is worth, measured so far** *[this session]*. The probe in §1.4 gives the neighbourhood sizes; a second probe (`swap_feasibility.py`) asks the decisive question for the **Pareto** arm — *does a conflicting partner have a free same-timing, same-hop-count lateral alternative that clears the newcomer's unimpeded plan while avoiding everyone else?* — using A\*'s own conservative raster with per-flight multiplicity (so a raster-feasible swap is ledger-feasible):

  | | 282 legs | 948 legs (≈21% FAA) | **4,636 legs (full FAA)** |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | (newcomer, earlier partner) pairs tested | 166 | 706 | **8,874** |
  | partner has a free same-timing swap | 57.8% | 53.0% | **27.7%** |
  | no same-timing geodesic alternative | 39.2% | 42.1% | 54.1% |
  | partner's filed path not a same-timing geodesic (outside the probe's reach) | 3.0% | 4.8% | 18.2% |
  | arrivals (with ≥ 1 partner) whose *every* partner can swap | 56.1% | 48.3% | **15.9%** |
  | arrivals with at least one swappable partner | 59.9% | 58.1% | 48.5% |
  | FCFS premium recoverable if every partner swaps (share of premium / of total cost) | 38.5% / 3.8% | 32.6% / 3.6% | **9.6% / 1.8%** |

  Read it plainly: **free lateral swaps of partners are real but thin at FAA density** — they would recover at most ~10% of the FCFS premium (~1.8% of cost) on their own, because at full density a newcomer usually has ≥ 2 partners and needs *all* of them to move, and more of the partners are themselves already detouring or holding. The rest of the 6–15% headroom lives in **paid partner moves** (a partner takes 1–2 steps of ground delay so the newcomer avoids 10) and in combined newcomer+partner moves — exactly what the utilitarian LNS2 repair searches and a Pareto-only rule forbids. This is consistent with the 600-flight colgen optimum, where 58% of flights are *held* and only 18% keep A\*'s filing. (The probe is an upper bound on the Pareto arm — it ignores swap–swap interactions — and a lower bound on the utilitarian arm — it ignores paid moves, newcomer-side combinations, and the 18% of partner paths it could not judge.)
- **Literature bracket.** Replan-all over FCFS ≈ 1% on sparse maps, 10–22% at dense intersections; moving ~5 agents recovers what moving ~30 would (SubID); Flatland partial replanning −19.9%; RHCR replan-all 4–7% throughput at 30–200× the runtime. Our FAA world is sparse mid-air and dense at hubs; our future world is dense.

### 4.5 Risks and design decisions specific to LNS2

- **Kernel soft mode.** A second occupancy layer (an owner-tagged interval pool) and a lexicographic (c, f) key in the A\* kernel; must be a separate entry point so the existing FCFS path stays byte-exact; bound the time axis (already `max_step`). The SIPPS route is the cost-aware `claude/sipp-compiled` kernel (multi-label Pareto frontier per cell-interval; ground delay in the start labels) plus the soft pool and `c` as the primary label key — the faster engine, but the branch is unmerged and was exact only at moderate density when last measured (saturation divergence noted in the memory), so re-validate before building on it.
- **Pad capacity and dwell.** Pad capacity is a counting constraint — keep it hard (SIPPS has no notion of "k-of-N soft"); endpoint dwell cylinders are just more soft/hard cells.
- **CP vs c.** Count exact colliding *pairs* on candidates (ledger `conflicts` / row claims); use the underestimating `c` inside the search as the paper does.
- **Determinism.** Iteration budgets and seeded RNG for neighbourhoods and priority orders — never wall-clock budgets inside the loop (colgen's pricing already lost cross-machine bit-identity that way). The reference code is a cautionary example, not a template: `rand()`/`std::random_shuffle`, a random tie-break *inside the heap comparator*, and every loop bounded by wall-clock (`cutoffTime`, per-replan `time_limit/100`), so it is seed-reproducible only for iteration-capped runs on one toolchain; DROP-LNS/WPPL are asynchronous and non-reproducible by construction — our parallel form must record the accepted sequence.
- **Mechanism and reporting.** Same as idea 2: re-filing a committed intent is a new mechanism; record the arm (`fcfs`, `fcfs+lns2-pareto`, `fcfs+lns2-utilitarian`), the per-arrival re-file log, and a fairness readout (reversals / time-order deviation, Chin et al.).
- **Plumbing.** Incremental `release`, occupancy un-raster, `TerminalCapacity` interval removal (shared with ideas 2 and 3A); the Track A envelope must cover released partners.

### 4.6 Verdict on idea 3

**This is the track to build.** It subsumes the salvageable part of idea 1 (LaCAM3's refiners *are* this loop) and gives idea 2 a better-scaling engine (PP + soft constraints instead of a constraint tree — the LNS2 authors tried PBS/Greedy-CBS as the neighbourhood re-planner and PP won). Two things the measurements add: the **Pareto (free-swap-only) arm is thin at FAA density** — ≤ ~10% of the FCFS premium, ~1.8% of cost — so the utilitarian repair (paid partner moves) is where the 6–15% headroom lives and it must be carried from day one with a fairness readout; and the repair must be **gated and bounded** (premium threshold τ, neighbourhood cap, iteration budget, freeze horizon) or it will replan uselessly and propagate. Suggested order: (1) incremental release plumbing; (2) kernel soft mode; (3) **offline Form A on the 948-leg cut, Pareto then utilitarian, differenced per flight against A\*** — the cheapest measurement of the headroom and a check of the swap-probe prediction; (4) online Form B per arrival, first sequential, then on the Track A runner; (5) hub-sharded cluster runs at future density. Report every arm next to the colgen IP number at 600 flights so the three methods sit on one ruler.
